SPY vs ES Futures: Which Suits Your Account?

Generational Wealth
September 3, 2026 · 8 min read

SPY and ES track the same index, so the choice is about size, not about view. SPY can be traded one share at a time, which lets a small account set a sensible risk per trade. One ES contract moves $12.50 per tick and cannot be divided, so it imposes a minimum risk you either can or cannot afford.

Almost every other difference — hours, cost, margin, tax treatment — follows from or is secondary to that one. Start with whether your account can carry the smallest available position, and most of the decision makes itself.

What each instrument actually is

SPY is the SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust, a unit investment trust launched on 22 January 1993 that holds the index constituents. It trades like any listed share, and it carries a gross expense ratio of 0.0945 percent a year (State Street Global Advisors, SPY fund page).

ES is the E-mini S&P 500 futures contract listed by CME Group. It is a cash-settled agreement on the index with a $50 multiplier and a minimum price increment of 0.25 index points, worth $12.50 (CME Group, E-mini S&P 500 contract specifications). It expires quarterly and has to be rolled, as futures contract rollover explains.

You are not choosing between two views of the market. You are choosing between two wrappers around the same exposure.

Granularity: the difference that actually decides it

This is the part worth being concrete about, because it is where most people choose wrongly.

With SPY, one share is the increment. If a setup gives you a stop distance of 80 cents and you are willing to risk $150 on the trade, you buy 187 shares. If the stop is wider, you buy fewer. The position adapts to the risk.

With ES, one contract is the increment and the risk adapts to the position. That same trade — measured in index points rather than cents — might imply a stop worth 8 points on one contract, which is $400. There is no fractional contract to shrink it with. Either $400 is an acceptable risk for that account or the trade cannot be taken at all.

For a five-figure account this is often the whole answer. A single ES contract routinely implies a risk per trade several times larger than a disciplined percentage of the account allows — the arithmetic is in how to size a position from risk, not conviction. That is precisely the gap the Micro E-mini was created to fill, at one tenth the multiplier and $1.25 a tick.

Cost: the honest comparison depends on your size

"Futures are cheaper" is repeated constantly and is only conditionally true.

The 0.0945 percent expense ratio is an annual figure accrued inside the fund. On an intraday position it is genuinely irrelevant — a fraction of a cent. What you actually pay in SPY is your broker's equity commission plus the spread, and in a name that liquid the spread is usually the narrowest available anywhere in the market.

Futures charge commission and exchange fees per contract with no ongoing holding cost. Because one contract carries a large notional exposure, the cost per dollar of exposure is low — which is exactly why the comparison flatters futures at large size and says nothing useful at small size. Two hundred shares of SPY versus one ES contract are not the same trade, and comparing their fees is not a comparison at all.

The trap in "cheaper per dollar of exposure." Cost efficiency at large size is only an advantage if you wanted large size. If a single contract forces you into exposure bigger than your plan allows, the low cost per dollar is measuring the efficiency of a mistake. Choose the size the account can support, then compare the cost of the instruments that offer it.

Hours, and what they change

ES trades nearly around the clock through the week, which means overnight moves happen while you can still act. That is a genuine advantage for anyone holding beyond a session: you are exposed to the move rather than to a gap.

SPY trades regular hours plus thin extended-hours sessions where spreads are far wider and several market protections do not operate — the mechanics are in pre-market vs regular hours. A position held overnight in SPY is exposed to a gap you cannot stop out of.

The honest caveat: near-24-hour access is only useful if you are awake and disciplined. For a trader with a day job it removes a real risk. For a trader who cannot leave a screen alone it adds sixteen hours of temptation, which is a worse problem than gap risk.

Margin and account rules after June 2026

The old shorthand — "you need $25,000 to day trade stocks, futures have no such rule" — is out of date. FINRA retired the pattern day trader framework and its $25,000 minimum equity requirement on 4 June 2026, replacing it with an intraday margin standard that applies to every margin account whether or not you day trade (FINRA Regulatory Notice 26-10). The detail, including why some brokers are still enforcing the old threshold through the phase-in period, is in the pattern day trader rule: what changed in 2026.

Futures work on a different basis entirely: exchange-set initial and maintenance margin per contract, with brokers offering reduced intraday day-trade margin at their own discretion. That is covered in futures margin explained. Low day-trade margin is not a discount — it is leverage, and it is the mechanism by which a single contract can produce a loss larger than the margin posted against it.

Tax treatment is not the same — and it is not our call

In the United States, futures contracts and equity ETFs fall under different tax regimes, and the difference can be material over a full year of active trading. We are not going to characterise your position: rules vary by jurisdiction and by individual circumstance, and this is exactly the kind of question where a wrong assumption is expensive. Ask a qualified tax professional before letting tax treatment influence which instrument you trade. Some of the general shape of the question is sketched in day trading taxes, again with the same caveat.

Which one suits which account — including who each is wrong for

  • SPY suits smaller accounts that need fine control over risk per trade, traders who want to scale in and out in small increments, and anyone whose broker relationship and platform are already equity-based.
  • SPY is wrong for traders who want overnight exposure they can manage continuously, and for anyone whose strategy needs the deep round-the-clock index liquidity that futures provide.
  • ES suits accounts large enough that one contract's implied risk is a small fraction of equity, traders who need the near-continuous session, and those who want symmetric long and short mechanics with no borrow.
  • ES is wrong for a small account, full stop. If one contract forces a risk per trade above your limit, the instrument does not fit the account and no amount of conviction changes that.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main difference between trading SPY and ES?

Granularity. SPY is an ETF you can buy one share at a time, so position size is almost infinitely adjustable and risk per trade can be tuned to any account. ES is a futures contract with a $50 multiplier, so the smallest position you can hold moves $12.50 per 0.25-point tick and cannot be subdivided. Both track the same index; only the size increment and the rule set differ.

Is ES cheaper to trade than SPY?

It depends entirely on the size you trade, not on the instrument. Futures charge a commission and exchange fees per contract with no holding cost, while SPY carries a gross expense ratio of 0.0945 percent per year plus your broker's equity commission. For a position held minutes the expense ratio is negligible; for equivalent large notional exposure the futures contract is usually the cheaper way to carry it.

Do you need $25,000 to day trade SPY?

Not any more. FINRA retired the pattern day trader framework and its $25,000 minimum equity requirement on June 4, 2026, replacing it with an intraday margin standard that applies to every margin account. Some brokers are still enforcing the old threshold during the phase-in period, so the practical answer depends on your firm rather than on the rule.

Should a beginner trade SPY or ES?

For a small account the sizing question usually decides it. SPY allows a position small enough that a normal stop risks a sensible fraction of the account, and a single ES contract often does not. Traders who want the futures session and structure but not the contract size generally use the Micro E-mini instead, which tracks the same index at one tenth the multiplier.

Bottom line

Both instruments give you the S&P 500. SPY gives it to you in pieces small enough to size properly; ES gives it to you in one block with a near-continuous session and no borrow to worry about. Decide by asking what one unit of each risks against your account, not by asking which is cheaper per dollar of exposure — that question assumes you wanted the exposure in the first place. If the answer is that ES is too big and SPY's hours are too short, the Micro E-mini is the option the comparison usually points at. For the contract mechanics in detail, see how to trade ES futures.
