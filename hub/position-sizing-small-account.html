Position Sizing for a Small Account

Generational Wealth
August 8, 2026 · 10 min read

Position sizing for a small account follows the same rule as any account: risk a fixed percentage per trade. The constraint is different. On a $2,000 account, 1% is $20, and many instruments cannot be traded that small. The job is to pick instruments whose minimum size fits your risk, not to raise your risk to fit the instrument.

That sentence is the whole article, and almost every small account that fails does so by inverting it. The trade is available, the capital is not quite enough, and the risk gets stretched to close the gap. Six weeks later the account is gone and the strategy gets the blame.

The rule does not change; the granularity does

The arithmetic is identical at every account size. Decide the percentage of capital you are willing to lose on one trade, divide it by the distance from entry to invalidation, and the answer is your position size. That process is set out in full in sizing a position from risk rather than conviction.

What changes on a small account is that the answer often comes back as a fraction of the smallest unit you can actually buy. You cannot trade 0.4 of a futures contract. When the maths says less than one, the correct output is not "round up" — it is a different instrument or no trade.

The Micro E-mini S&P 500 is one-tenth the size of the standard E-mini, with a contract multiplier of $5 per index point and a minimum tick of 0.25 points worth $1.25 (CME Group, Micro E-mini S&P 500 contract specifications). That single design decision is what put index futures within reach of a four-figure account at a sane risk level; before micros existed, the honest advice to a $5,000 account was to trade something else.

Read the right-hand column as a floor, not a target. If your account is below the figure listed, that instrument cannot be traded at 1% risk with that stop — no technique changes it.

Small account, same percentage. The most common small-account mistake is treating the percentage as negotiable because the dollar amount feels trivial. Losing $20 on a $2,000 account is exactly as significant as losing $2,000 on a $200,000 account — the percentages are what compound. Ten losses at 1% costs you just under 10% of the account. Ten losses at 5% costs you 40% and needs a 67% gain to undo. The rule that keeps this bounded is the 1% rule.

Four legitimate ways to make a trade fit

  1. Trade the smaller instrument. Micro futures instead of minis, micro lots instead of mini lots, fractional shares instead of whole ones. This is the first answer and it solves most cases outright.
  2. Take the setup with the tighter invalidation. A structurally valid stop three points away rather than twelve is not a smaller stop — it is a different, better-located trade. The invalidation must still be somewhere the idea is genuinely wrong, which is the whole subject of setting a stop that is not a guess.
  3. Reduce the number of open positions instead of their size. One position at full planned risk beats three at a third each, because the three are rarely independent — see correlation risk.
  4. Skip it. "No trade" is a position size, and on a small account it is the one that gets used least and earns most. There will be another setup this week.

What the 2026 rule change means for small accounts

For twenty-five years, the binding constraint on a small US stock-trading account was the pattern day trader rule and its $25,000 equity threshold. FINRA retired that framework on June 4, 2026 and replaced it with an intraday margin standard that applies to every margin account rather than counting day trades (FINRA Regulatory Notice 26-10).

Two practical points follow. First, the old threshold no longer decides whether a small account can day trade stocks, which removes the main historic reason small accounts migrated to futures and forex. Second, firms may phase the change in and are free to keep stricter house rules, so the number that binds you is your broker's, not FINRA's — check before you plan around it. The detail is in the pattern day trader rule explained.

Costs weigh more at this size

A $6 round turn is 0.3% of a $2,000 account and 0.003% of a $200,000 one. Identical trade, hundredfold difference in drag. On a small account, commissions, spread and slippage are a genuine component of expectancy rather than a rounding error, and they push in one direction: fewer, better trades.

  • Count costs inside your expectancy, not beside it — the method is in expectancy.
  • Prefer instruments with tight spreads at the times you actually trade, rather than the times the marketing screenshots were taken.
  • Watch minimum commissions, which fall hardest on small positions and can make a micro-sized trade uneconomic even when the risk fits.

The shortcuts to refuse

Each of these is a way of getting a bigger position without more capital, and each one trades a survivable account for a faster ending.

  • Raising leverage to compensate for size. Leverage changes what your capital can control, not whether your strategy makes money. See what leverage actually is.
  • Trading without a stop so the position "cannot" be stopped out. The loss still happens; you have only removed your say in how big it is.
  • Averaging down into a losing position, which converts a defined 1% loss into an undefined one.
  • One big swing to get to a "real" account size. The distribution of outcomes for that plan is well documented in the mistakes that blow up trading accounts.

Frequently Asked Questions

What percentage should I risk per trade on a small account?

The same percentage you would use on a large one, typically 1% or less. Small accounts are not a reason to risk more; they are a reason to trade smaller instruments. Risking 5% because 1% feels like too little money is how a small account becomes no account, since twenty consecutive losses at 5% is a realistic sequence over a first year.

What is the smallest account that can trade futures properly?

With Micro E-mini contracts, a 10-point stop on the Micro E-mini S&P 500 risks about 50 dollars, which is 1% of a 5,000 dollar account. Below roughly 4,000 to 5,000 dollars, most index futures stops no longer fit inside a 1% rule, and micro-lot forex or fractional shares give finer control.

Do I still need $25,000 to day trade US stocks?

Not under the current rule. FINRA retired the pattern day trader framework on June 4, 2026 and replaced it with an intraday margin standard that applies to all margin accounts. Firms may phase the change in until October 2027 and may keep stricter house requirements, so the threshold that binds you is your own broker's, not FINRA's.

Should I use more leverage to make a small account grow faster?

No. Leverage changes how large a position your capital can control; it does not change your edge, and it multiplies the drawdown from an ordinary losing run. The way a small account grows is by surviving long enough for a positive expectancy to compound, which requires the risk per trade to stay small precisely when it feels least satisfying.

Bottom line

A small account does not need a different risk rule; it needs instruments fine-grained enough to obey the rule it already has. Work out the risk on one unit at your normal stop, compare it to 1% of your capital, and let that decide what you trade — micros, micro lots, fractional shares, or nothing today. Keep costs inside your expectancy, check your broker's own margin requirements rather than assuming the old thresholds, and resist every shortcut that makes the position bigger without making the edge better. The wider framework is risk management in trading, and the realistic starting-capital question is answered in how much money you need to start day trading.
